KINSTATE

The Missing Link: Effect of Inhibitor Structure on Kinase Conformational State Affinity, Kinase Selectivity and Efficacy

 

 

1. Excellence

 

1.1  State of the art, knowledge needs and project objectives

Every year, promising drug candidates fail in cells despite potent activity in the test tube. For the type III receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), which include CSF1R, KIT, FLT3 and PDGFRα/β, this failure is systematic and reproducible, and it has only been partly explained. The accepted account is that the high intracellular ATP concentration outcompetes ATP-competitive inhibitors relative to the conditions of an enzymatic assay. That covers part of the loss. It does not explain why two inhibitors of the same kinase, profiled in identical assays at matched ATP concentrations, translate from enzyme to cell with efficiencies that differ by more than an order of magnitude. We started KINSTATE because we saw exactly that discrepancy in our own laboratory, and we believe the missing piece is the conformational state of the kinase.

The observation that initiated this project

The work behind KINSTATE comes from a medicinal chemistry and drug discovery group at the Department of Chemistry, NTNU, which the two applicants have run together for more than twenty years. Our CSF1R programme started over a decade ago as an offshoot of our EGFR chemistry and has since produced more than 250 compounds across two scaffolds, the 4,6-disubstituted pyrrolo[2,3-d]pyrimidines and a purine series [1,7,8], a co-crystal structure of an inhibitor bound to the autoinhibited kinase domain (PDB 8CGC) [7] and two patent applications filed in 2023. Synthesis and some enzymology are done in-house, while ADME and in vivo pharmacology are done with the Lead Discovery Center in Dortmund. Molecular modelling has been part of the programme from the start: docking, pharmacophore modelling, conformational analysis and in silico screening decide which compounds we make, and that modelling work is what produced the observation KINSTATE is built on.

In developing CSF1R inhibitors we profiled our compounds alongside the approved drug pexidartinib. Our inhibitors reach sub-nanomolar enzymatic IC₅₀ values, ten to fifty times more potent than pexidartinib, yet they need 1–3 µM for activity in cells, while pexidartinib reaches its cellular endpoint at 0.064 µM (Figure 2). Two quantities follow from this, and they should be kept apart. The absolute enzymatic-to-cellular gap for our best compounds exceeds 1,000-fold, against about 6-fold for pexidartinib. More diagnostically, the translation efficiency, meaning how faithfully the enzymatic rank order survives into cells, differs by roughly 25-fold between the two sets. A compound far more potent on the isolated enzyme is far weaker in the cell. We tested three standard explanations for this difference and excluded all of them.

Enzymatic IC₅₀ values were measured at KM (~10 µM), 25 µM and 2.5 mM ATP. Our inhibitors held low-nanomolar potency at all three concentrations, and pexidartinib lost only about 4-fold at 2.5 mM. Correcting for the cellular ATP concentration leaves a residual gap of 100- to 500-fold for our compounds against less than 10-fold for pexidartinib [1], so ATP competition cannot account for the difference. Permeability and efflux do no better: MDCK assays gave Papp(A→B) of 15–38 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s and efflux ratios below 2.6 for the most active compounds, against Papp 4.5 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s for pexidartinib [ES1] [1,2]. Compounds that cross the membrane more readily than pexidartinib cannot be weaker in cells for reasons of permeability. General toxicity is also excluded: in the IL-3-driven Ba/F3 viability control the compounds were non-toxic above 9.9 µM, well above their CSF1R cellular IC₅₀ values [1,7,8].

We therefore looked for a structural mechanism, and two independent lines of evidence converged. Hybrid compounds that docked well against our autoinhibited CSF1R co-crystal structure (PDB 8CGC) were enzymatically inactive, whereas docking against the non-autoinhibited pexidartinib-bound structure (PDB 4R7H) reproduced activity for pexidartinib-like compounds but not for ours [2]. A triazole inhibitor docked with near-equal affinity into both structures, but in inverted orientations, with different pharmacophoric groups engaging the pocket depending on the receptor conformation [3]. Both results point the same way: our inhibitors and pexidartinib engage different conformational states of CSF1R. In cytokine-stimulated cells the equilibrium moves toward the active conformation, so our inhibitors meet a receptor that has largely left the state they bind most tightly.

The conformational states of CSF1R and the CSPR concept

Like all type III RTKs, CSF1R exists as a conformational ensemble, and four of its states matter pharmacologically (Figure 1). In State I the receptor is autoinhibited and DFG-out: the juxtamembrane (JM) domain folds back over the kinase domain and locks the activation loop in a catalytically inactive orientation, with the DFG motif (Asp-Phe-Gly, at the entrance to the ATP site) in the out rotamer. ATP affinity is negligible (Kd > 1,000 µM). This is the BBAminus macrostate in the Kincore nomenclature [5]. In State II the receptor is no longer autoinhibited but DFG is still out: cytokine-driven dimerisation triggers JM phosphorylation and release, the active site opens and ATP affinity rises sharply (Kd = 16 µM). State II is the primary binding conformation for pexidartinib and vimseltinib [4]. In State III, ATP binding drives the DFG flip to in and completes activation, giving the catalytically competent BLAminus receptor of the fully stimulated cell. State IV is the same DFG-in form locked by activation-loop phosphorylation (Y807), and activation-loop mutations such as KIT D816V hold the receptor there [6].

 

Figure 1. [ES2] The four pharmacologically relevant conformational states of CSF1R. State I (autoinhibited, DFG-out): JM domain docked over the kinase domain, preferred by the group's pyrrolopyrimidine and purine inhibitors. State II (non-autoinhibited, DFG-out): JM released after cytokine-driven phosphorylation, preferred by pexidartinib and vimseltinib. States III and IV: DFG-in active and locked forms. CSPR (conformational state preference ratio) = Kd(State II)/Kd(State I), measured against two recombinant constructs: a full-length kinase domain with intact JM domain (Y538–S939; State I mimic) and a truncated construct lacking the JM element (I564–S939; State II mimic). Standard enzymatic IC₅₀ assays use only the truncated (State II) construct and cannot report on State I binding. JM = juxtamembrane domain; pY = phosphotyrosine.

 

We define the conformational state preference ratio (CSPR) as Kd(State II)/Kd(State I), the factor by which an inhibitor binds the autoinhibited conformation more tightly than the non-autoinhibited one. It is determined against two recombinant CSF1R kinase-domain constructs: a full-length construct with the intact JM domain (residues Y538–S939), which stabilises and thereby models the autoinhibited State I, and a truncated construct lacking the JM element (residues I564–S939), which models the non-autoinhibited State II [4]. The proxy rests on the structural role of the JM domain. In the full-length construct the JM element docks intramolecularly and enforces the autoinhibited geometry, as it does in the intact resting receptor, and its removal in the truncated construct relieves that constraint. Inhibitor Kd against each construct is obtained by competitive enzymatic assay. A CSPR above 1 indicates State I preference and a CSPR below 1 indicates State II preference. These constructs let us measure binding to a conformation the standard single-construct enzymatic assay cannot see, which is why a compound can look potent enzymatically, against State I, and weak in cells, where States II and III dominate.

Using this assay we found that our pyrrolopyrimidine and purine inhibitors are the first inhibitors reported to prefer the autoinhibited State I of CSF1R, with CSPR above 50 for the Type A pyrrolopyrimidines [7] and 72 for purine compound 9 [8]. Pexidartinib has the opposite preference (CSPR ≈ 0.016; 360 nM against State I, 5.8 nM against State II) [4], as do all previously characterised CSF1R inhibitors (imatinib, dasatinib, GW-2580) [4,9]. Industry has independently validated State-selective targeting: vimseltinib (FDA-approved 2025) achieves about 100-fold DFG-out preference, which is credited for its selectivity across the kinome [10,11]. What nobody has established is the quantitative relationship between CSPR and cellular or in vivo efficacy, and that is the gap KINSTATE fills.

Hatched bars: cellular IC₅₀ (Ba/F3 CSF1R). Pexidartinib (CSPR ≈ 0.016) shows a gap of about 6-fold, while the Type A pyrrolopyrimidine (CSPR > 50) and purine 9 (CSPR = 72) show gaps above 1,000-fold. The conformational mismatch hypothesis

The evidence converges on one model. In cytokine-stimulated cells the receptor equilibrium shifts toward States II to IV, so inhibitors with high CSPR, which bind State I tightly, face a receptor population in which their preferred conformation is the minority. Conformational mismatch explains the different cellular behaviour of our inhibitors and of pexidartinib without invoking permeability, efflux or ATP, and it makes a falsifiable prediction: for high-CSPR inhibitors the cellular potency gap should grow with cytokine concentration, while for low-CSPR compounds it should stay flat. WP4 tests this directly.

The problem is not confined to our compounds or to CSF1R. KIT, FLT3 and PDGFRα/β share the JM autoinhibitory mechanism and the same four states, and inhibitor programmes against these kinases in gastrointestinal stromal tumour, acute myeloid leukaemia and myeloproliferative disorders face the same unrecognised problem. A framework that explains and predicts the CSPR–efficacy relationship for CSF1R will transfer across the family.

Project objectives

KINSTATE tests the hypothesis that CSPR is a primary structural determinant of (i) kinase selectivity, (ii) the magnitude of the enzymatic/cellular potency gap, and (iii) in vivo microglial depletion efficacy. The project will:

•     Map the CSF1R conformational transition by MD simulation, the first such study, to resolve at atomic level how inhibitor structure determines CSPR (WP1)

•     Establish a quantitative SAR of CSPR across three inhibitor series spanning >60 new compounds, guided by WP1 predictions (WP2–WP3)

•     Deliver the first systematic CSPR dataset for CSF1R inhibitors, including all approved agents, and test residence time as a predictor of cellular potency (WP3)

•     Test the conformational mismatch hypothesis directly in primary macrophages by shifting the State I/State II equilibrium through graded cytokine stimulation (WP4)

•     Validate the CSPR–efficacy relationship in vivo using an established murine microglial depletion model (WP5)

 

1.2  Research questions and hypotheses, theoretical approach and methodology

Five work packages build sequentially from structural understanding (WP1) through molecular design (WP2–WP3) to cellular (WP4) and in vivo (WP5) validation. Three structurally defined inhibitor series span the CSPR continuum and give us the experimental handles.

Type A compounds are 4,6-disubstituted pyrrolo[2,3-d]pyrimidines, with compound 23 (PDB 8CGC) as the primary scaffold. C-4 carries an N-methyl-N-benzylamine in the front pocket and C-6 an aryl group that extends into a hydrophobic back pocket open only in State I. They have CSPR above 50, enzymatic IC₅₀ below 3 nM, cellular (Ba/F3) IC₅₀ of 1–3 µM and high selectivity (S-score 0.04). These are the high-CSPR extreme.

Type B compounds are sp3-enriched and hybrid pyrrolopyrimidines, in which the C-4 aromatic amine is replaced by an aliphatic tetrahydropyran (THP) amine (Fsp³ 0.10 → 0.37) and the C-6 aryl by partially saturated or pexidartinib-derived dipyridyl groups. Representatives are 16t (BMDM IC₅₀ about 83 nM) and 12b (enzymatic IC₅₀ 1.54 nM). Their CSPR is intermediate and variable and their cellular potency is better, so they should populate the middle of the CSPR–efficacy correlation. Without them we would have two points and no curve.

Type C is REVIE-001, a structurally distinct, brain-penetrant CSF1R inhibitor whose CSPR is unknown at the outset. Measuring it (WP3) and placing Type C on the same CSPR–efficacy axis as Types A and B is a deliberate out-of-class validation: if a chemically unrelated scaffold obeys the same relationship, the relationship belongs to the target and not to one chemotype. Type C is also the only series with documented CNS penetration, which makes it the lead for the WP5 neuroinflammation endpoint.

The central prediction is a negative correlation between CSPR and translation efficiency across all three series: the higher the State I preference, the larger the enzymatic-to-cellular gap. Types A, B and C give the high, intermediate and out-of-class points needed to test this as a quantitative relationship.

WP1: MD simulation of the CSF1R conformational transition (Years 1–2)

Research question: what is the free-energy pathway of the BBAminus ↔ BLAminus transition in CSF1R, and which inhibitor structural features stabilise State I?

No MD simulation of the CSF1R DFG-in/out transition exists. The DFG motif is the switch: in the autoinhibited receptor the Phe side chain rotates outward (DFG-out) and opens a hydrophobic back pocket unique to that state, and on activation it rotates inward (DFG-in), collapsing the pocket and completing the catalytic site. In Kincore terms, DFG-out with the αC helix displaced is the BBAminus macrostate (State I) and DFG-in with αC engaged is BLAminus (State III). Locking the receptor in BBAminus is what our high-CSPR inhibitors do. The dual binding mode of compound 27a, with near-equal docking affinity (−10.8 kcal/mol) into both 8CGC and 4R7H but in inverted orientations [3], shows that the pocket geometry changes substantially along the transition. Starting from the experimental endpoints 8CGC (State I) and 3LCD (State III), we will map the transition by metadynamics with the DFG χ1 dihedral and Phe797 displacement as collective variables, as validated for Abl and Src [12,13]. AlphaFold2 models are excluded because of their strong DFG-in bias [14]. The Phe of the DFG motif is also RS2 of the regulatory spine, the four-residue hydrophobic stack (RS1 to RS4) that holds the DFG motif and the αC helix in register in the catalytically competent kinase. The same trajectories therefore report on spine assembly. We will track the RS1 to RS4 contact distances along the transition coordinate and record when the spine breaks relative to the opening of the back pocket. That gives a structural measure, next to the binding energy, of how far into BBAminus an inhibitor drives the receptor. Types A, B and C will be docked into trajectory snapshots and MM-GBSA binding energies computed along the coordinate, which yields CSPR predictions for experimental validation in WP3.

WP2: Synthesis and SAR of conformational state preference (Years 1–3)

Research question: which structural features determine CSPR, and can a target CSPR be reached by design?

Four design principles from more than 250 existing compounds guide the synthesis. C-6 substitution is required, since C-4 and C-5 analogues are inactive despite good 8CGC docking [2], consistent with C-6 engaging the State I-specific back pocket. Partial planarity at C-6 is necessary: cyclohexene retains potency and cyclohexyl does not (60° against 20° torsion) [1]. A THP amine at C-4 outperforms aromatic amines in 9 of 11 matched pairs [1]. And compound 27a shows that one scaffold can reach both states [3]. R-spine contact is a fourth descriptor, next to Fsp³ and the C-6 torsion. The C-6 aryl group enters the back pocket that opens when Phe797 swings out, the space RS2 itself vacates, and from there it can reach RS3 on the αC helix. How many spine residues a compound touches can be read off 8CGC and 4R7H and predicted for new compounds from the WP1 trajectories [22], so we can design against it. We will make about 25 compounds a year across Types A, B and C, with WP1 predictions guiding the choice of substituents, and profile all of them enzymatically at KM and 2.5 mM ATP and by ADME assays before progression.

WP3: Measurement of CSPR and kinase selectivity (Years 1–3)

Research question: does CSPR predict kinase selectivity and cellular potency across structurally diverse inhibitors, and does residence time add predictive power?

CSPR will be determined for all of the more than 50 WP2 compounds against the two constructs (Y538–S939 for State I, I564–S939 for State II) by competitive enzymatic assay following Wodicka et al. [4], a method our group has applied to both the pyrrolopyrimidine and the purine series [7,8]. It will be the largest CSPR dataset for any kinase inhibitor series. Reference values for pexidartinib, vimseltinib and pimicotinib, whose preference is undocumented, anchor the scale. We will also test whether residence time predicts cellular potency better than equilibrium Kd. Residence time (τ = 1/koff) is obtained by a jump-dilution competition kinetics assay: inhibitor-saturated enzyme is rapidly diluted into substrate and the single-exponential recovery of activity is fitted to extract koff. This is the standard method for slow-binding inhibitors [15,16] and it has never been applied to a CSF1R inhibitor. A 46-kinase panel (competitive binding, Eurofins DiscoverX [17]) gives kinome-wide selectivity scores and tests whether CSPR and selectivity co-vary. R-spine engagement may tie the two measurements together. In a kinetic profile of 172 inhibitors across the autoinhibited, non-autoinhibited and D816V states of KIT, compounds without R-spine contact had a median residence time of 0.4 min, against 159 min for compounds touching three spine residues, and their association was slower in proportion [22]. The same lever has been used to tune residence time for p38α and BTK inhibitors [23,24]. Our hypothesis is that spine contact causes both: a compound that occupies the position of a displaced spine residue has to bind the state in which that residue is out, which gives high CSPR, and has to dissociate slowly, which gives long τ. We will test this by regressing cellular potency on CSPR, τ and R-spine contact count. If contact count correlates with both while CSPR and τ carry no independent weight, one structural parameter accounts for the pair. Spine contact travels with back-cleft binding, which on its own gives slow kon and long τ. The Type B series, where the C-6 group varies but the binding site does not, lets us separate the two.

WP4: Cellular test of the conformational mismatch hypothesis (Years 2–3)

Research question: does the enzymatic/cellular potency gap increase as CSF1R is driven from the autoinhibited to the active conformation by physiological cytokine stimulation, independently of ATP and permeability?

Ba/F3 cells engineered for CSF1R dependence rely on constitutively active receptor signalling and their receptor state cannot be titrated with exogenous ligand, so they are unsuitable here. We will use primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), which express CSF1R at physiological levels and respond predictably to CSF-1 withdrawal and restimulation in an assay already established in our laboratory [8]. BMDMs are isolated from C57BL/6J femurs and matured. For the state titration the cells are cytokine-starved for 24 h, which shifts them toward State I, pre-treated with inhibitor for 30 min, then stimulated with CSF-1 at 0, 1, 10 or 100 ng/mL for 10 min, spanning near-resting to fully activated receptor. The readout is pERK1/2 normalised to total ERK1/2 [8], and IC₅₀ curves are built at each CSF-1 level for five Type A compounds (high CSPR), five Type B compounds (intermediate CSPR) and pexidartinib (low CSPR). CSF1R signalling maintains tissue macrophages and microglia through this MAPK axis [18], so pERK1/2 is a physiologically faithful proxy for target engagement. Companion MDCK permeability on the same panel excludes concentration-dependent membrane artefacts. Our model predicts that Type A potency falls steeply as CSF-1 rises while pexidartinib and the low-CSPR Type B compounds stay flat; the ATP hypothesis predicts uniform behaviour at every CSF-1 level. The decisive test is a regression of M-CSF sensitivity, the slope of IC₅₀ against CSF-1, on CSPR across all eleven compounds. The panel spans R-spine contact count as well as CSPR, so the same regression can be run against either descriptor, or against both.

WP5: In vivo validation (Years 3–4)

Research question: does CSPR predict in vivo microglial depletion potency?

CSF1R inhibition depletes up to 99% of microglia in mice [19], a clean in vivo endpoint that reflects target engagement. To power a genuine CSPR–efficacy correlation, six compounds spanning the CSPR range (two Type A, two Type B, one Type C and pexidartinib) will each be dosed at three levels in C57BL/6J mice (n = 6 per group, both sexes) by oral gavage for 14 days, and an ED₅₀ for microglial depletion determined for each. Brain tissue is analysed by flow cytometry (CD45/CD11b/Tmem119/MHC II/CD86/CD206), gating CD45^low/Tmem119⁺ as homeostatic microglia and CD45^high/CD11b⁺ as infiltrating macrophages, with the Tmem119 level as a graded engagement marker [20,21]. The primary analysis correlates in vivo depletion ED₅₀ with WP3 CSPR and with the BMDM M-CSF sensitivity from WP4, powered across six compounds and three doses to detect a Spearman ρ of 0.8 or more at α = 0.05. The six compounds also span R-spine contact count, and a secondary analysis correlates ED₅₀ with that count, to see whether the structural descriptor predicts in vivo potency as well as the measured CSPR does. Brain exposure (free drug) is measured for every compound so that potency is read against the concentration at the target site.

Risk management

Four risks need managing. MD convergence is met with several enhanced-sampling protocols and collective variables, cross-validated against the Abl and Src literature, and WP2 to WP5 do not depend on WP1 finishing on schedule. If WP4 returns a null result, with Type A and Type B showing identical CSF-1 sensitivity, the hypothesis is falsified, but the BMDM protocol still resolves whether ATP, permeability or another mechanism dominates, which is publishable and actionable. The assumption that the full-length and truncated constructs faithfully report State I and State II will be cross-checked against the WP1 simulations and the WP3 structural overlay (Figure 3); a divergence would itself tell us something about the limits of the enzymatic proxy. Poor in vivo PK is met with ADME-by-design in WP2 and with PLX5622, whose PK is established, as a WP5 benchmark while our own compounds are optimised.

 

1.3  Ethical issues

The main ethical consideration is the use of mice in WP5. Approval from the Norwegian Food Safety Authority will be obtained before any animal work, in full compliance with EU Directive 2010/63/EU and the Norwegian Animal Welfare Act. We apply the 3Rs throughout. Replacement is not possible, because no validated in vitro model reproduces in vivo microglial depletion. Reduction comes from power-calculated group sizes and from a within-study dose-response design that reuses control groups across compounds. Refinement comes from daily welfare monitoring and pre-defined humane endpoints. The power analysis shows that n = 6 per dose group is enough to detect a 30% change in microglial depletion (α = 0.05, 80% power) and a Spearman ρ of 0.8 for the CSPR–efficacy correlation. No human subjects, human tissue or CMR category 1–2 compounds are involved.

 

1.4  Novelty and ambition

KINSTATE addresses a question the field has left open for decades: why do structurally similar kinase inhibitors differ so much in how faithfully enzymatic potency translates into cellular and in vivo activity? We give a specific, testable mechanistic answer, conformational state mismatch, and an experimental programme built to confirm or refute it. Five advances beyond the state of the art follow.

The MD simulation of the CSF1R DFG-in/out transition will be the first, despite the clinical importance of the receptor, and it supplies the atomic-level basis for how inhibitor structure controls CSPR, which static crystal structures cannot give. The quantitative SAR of conformational state preference is also the first: no study has systematically mapped chemical structure to CSPR for any kinase, and we will do it for three scaffolds, yielding principles that carry over to the type III RTK family. The CSPR dataset, covering more than 50 compounds together with all three approved agents, treats CSPR as a design parameter instead of an incidental observation, and it carries the first test of residence time as a predictor of cellular potency for this class. The BMDM CSF-1 gradient is the first controlled cellular test of conformational mismatch, and the only design that can separate mismatch from ATP competition and permeability in a physiologically relevant primary cell; the hypothesis has been speculated about [2,4,8] and never tested. Finally, correlating CSPR with microglial depletion in vivo ties a quantitative molecular descriptor to a CNS pharmacology endpoint, which bears directly on CSF1R inhibitors for neuroinflammatory disease.

If this is confirmed, CSPR becomes a standard design parameter alongside enzymatic potency and ADME, and every type III RTK programme will have to approach the biochemical-to-cellular step differently. That step currently fails the field often and for reasons nobody has pinned down. The risk matches the ambition: even a null WP4 result resolves for the first time which mechanism dominates the enzymatic/cellular gap and gives actionable design guidance.
